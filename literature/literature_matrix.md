## 1. Verified Literature Matrix

| Author(s) | Year | Country / Context | Dataset | Method | Variables | Main Findings | Limitations | Relevance to My Study | Type |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Thomas, Crook, & Edelman** | 2002 | Global / Banking | Retail bank loan portfolios | Weight of Evidence (WoE), Logistic Regression, Scorecard construction | Demographics, income, past delinquent events, loan purpose | Establishes foundational mathematics for credit scoring, logistic scorecard scaling, and odds ratios. | Assumes static formal bureau history; fails for thin-file borrowers. | Baseline scorecard standard for Objective 1 and Chapter 2. | Peer-Reviewed Book |
| **Hand & Henley** | 1997 | United Kingdom / Consumer Credit | Consumer lending portfolios | Statistical classification review (DA, Logistic, k-NN, CART) | Financial attributes, employment duration, credit history | Demonstrates that standard accuracy is inadequate; cost of misclassifying bads is far higher than goods. | Written before modern tree boosting (XGBoost) and big data. | Theoretical justification for asymmetric loss and FNR focus. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-985X.1997.00078.x) |
| **Stiglitz & Weiss** | 1981 | United States / Economic Theory | Microeconomic theoretical equilibrium model | Information asymmetry and credit rationing mathematical model | Interest rates, borrower collateral, project risk distribution | Proves that raising interest rates causes adverse selection and moral hazard, leading to rationing of creditworthy borrowers. | Purely theoretical; does not provide an empirical prediction algorithm. | Core economic theoretical foundation for why thin-file Namibians are excluded. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (AER, 71(3), 393–410) |
| **Lessmann, Baesens, Seow, & Thomas** | 2015 | International (8 countries) | 8 public/commercial credit bureau datasets | Large-scale algorithm benchmarking (Logit, Random Forest, XGBoost, Neural Nets) | Bureau credit histories, revolving credit, financial ratios | Ensemble methods (Gradient Boosting, Random Forest) statistically outperform Logistic Regression on ROC-AUC and H-measure. | Benchmarked exclusively on traditional bureau data, not mobile/alternative data. | Justifies model selection (Logistic Regression vs. Random Forest vs. XGBoost). | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1016/j.ejor.2015.05.030) |
| **Björkegren & Grissen** | 2020 | South America | Proprietary call detail records (CDR) matched to microloans ($N \approx 5,000$) | Feature engineering + regularized regression/ensembles | Call frequency, timing, network dispersion, recharge regularity | Behavioral mobile signatures predict default at least as well as credit bureau models for thin-file individuals. | Proprietary telecom data not open-source; South American tariff context differs from Africa. | Primary empirical proof that mobile footprints act as viable credit proxies. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1093/wber/lhz003) |
| **Óskarsdóttir, Bravo, Sarraute, Vanthienen, & Baesens** | 2019 | Latin America | Telecom CDR combined with formal bank application data ($N > 10,000$) | Social network analysis and ML classifiers (Logistic, Random Forest) | Call graph centrality, reciprocity, call volume, loan attributes | Adding calling behavior to traditional scoring models increases AUC and produces the most profitable portfolio. | Profitability conclusions tied to specific lender cost structure. | Validates combining alternative transaction features with applicant context. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1016/j.asoc.2018.10.004) |
| **Suri & Jack** | 2016 | Kenya | Long-run national household panel (FinAccess 2008–2014) | Difference-in-differences panel regression | M-Pesa agent proximity, mobile wallet usage, consumption, poverty level | Mobile money access lifted 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty and enabled women to transition from subsistence to business. | Focuses on economic welfare and consumption smoothing rather than credit default prediction. | Establishes the real socio-economic welfare justification for digital financial inclusion. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1126/science.aah5309) |
| **Mwesigwa, Jackson, & Csikszentmihalyi** | 2026 | Nairobi, Kenya | Qualitative 9-month ethnography of digital lending (M-Shwari) | Socio-technical ethnography and institutional analysis | Algorithmic scoring workflows, CDR logs, collector practices | Shows how alternative-data scoring is institutionalized in practice; warns against predatory micro-loan cycling. | Qualitative study; does not publish quantitative model weights. | Direct evidence for the necessity of consumer safeguards in digital lending. | Peer-Reviewed Conference (ACM CHI '26) |
| **Lundberg & Lee** | 2017 | United States / Computer Science | Benchmark ML datasets (Census, NHANES) | Cooperative game theory (Shapley values) unified into SHAP | Multi-modal feature vectors | Proves SHAP is the only local feature attribution method satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency. | Exact computation is NP-hard without tree optimizations. | Mathematical foundation for model explainability in Objective 4 / SRQ 3. | Peer-Reviewed Conference (NeurIPS 2017, 30, 4765–4774) |
| **Lundberg et al.** | 2020 | United States / Computational Biology | Medical & financial tree ensembles | TreeSHAP algorithm (polynomial-time Shapley calculation) | Non-linear tabular features | Enables exact, polynomial-time Shapley value computation for gradient boosted trees and random forests. | Focuses on tree ensembles; does not apply directly to neural nets without sampling. | Technical engine used for XGBoost and Random Forest explainability in this study. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1038/s42256-019-0138-9) |
| **Ribeiro, Singh, & Guestrin** | 2016 | United States / Computer Science | Image & tabular classification benchmarks | Sparse local linear surrogate modeling (LIME) | Interpretable localized feature perturbations | Explains individual predictions by approximating black-box models locally with an interpretable surrogate. | Local sampling instability; can produce differing explanations across repeated runs. | Used alongside SHAP for case-by-case loan adverse-action disclosures. | Peer-Reviewed Conference (ACM KDD 2016, 1135–1144) |
| **Misheva, Osterrieder, Hirsa, Kulkarni, & Lin** | 2021 | Global / Fintech | LendingClub peer-to-peer loan dataset ($N > 100,000$) | Comparative XAI (TreeSHAP, KernelSHAP, LIME) across ML models | Debt-to-income, revolving line, credit inquiries, FICO | Shows SHAP and LIME are complementary: SHAP gives stable global feature rankings while LIME provides fast localized adverse reasons. | Conducted on traditional US peer-to-peer data, not African mobile money. | Directly informs our dual SHAP + LIME explainability pipeline architecture. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.3389/frai.2021.683032) |
| **Mehrabi, Morstatter, Saxena, Lerman, & Galstyan** | 2021 | United States / Computer Science | Survey across ML benchmarks | Algorithmic fairness taxonomy and bias survey | Protected attributes (gender, race, age), outcome labels | Comprehensive taxonomy of bias sources (historical, representation, measurement) and mitigation strategies. | Broad survey; does not focus exclusively on banking regulation. | Theoretical foundation for fairness auditing and proxy discrimination checks. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1145/3457607) |
| **Hardt, Price, & Srebro** | 2016 | United States / Machine Learning | FICO credit score dataset | Mathematical formulation of Equalized Odds and Equal Opportunity | Protected attributes, true labels, predicted loan outcomes | Proves that demographic parity can harm qualified minorities; Equal Opportunity (equal TPR) protects creditworthy individuals. | Requires access to the true ground truth outcome label $Y$. | Guides our selection of fairness metrics: Disparate Impact vs. Equal Opportunity. | Peer-Reviewed Conference (NeurIPS 2016, 3315–3323) |
| **Beck & Demirguc-Kunt** | 2006 | Cross-Country (Developing Economies) | World Bank Enterprise Surveys (thousands of SMEs) | Econometric cross-country regression | Firm size, collateral requirements, financing obstacles, sales growth | Small and medium enterprises face significantly higher credit constraints; lack of financing directly retards growth. | Cross-sectional survey data; does not evaluate machine learning models. | Academic evidence establishing why informal/SME credit access is vital for national growth. | Peer-Reviewed Journal (DOI: 10.1016/j.jbankfin.2006.05.009) |
| **Demirgüç-Kunt, Klapper, Singer, & Ansar** | 2022 | Global / Sub-Saharan Africa | World Bank Global Findex Database (125,000+ adults across 123 countries) | Nationally representative survey cross-tabulations | Account ownership, mobile money adoption, digital payments, borrowing sources | Sub-Saharan Africa is the global leader in mobile money (33% of adults), but formal borrowing remains critically low. | Macro survey; does not contain individual loan repayment logs. | Grounds the macro demand-side reality of African digital financial services. | Policy / World Bank Report (DOI: 10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4) |
| **Namibia Statistics Agency (NSA)** | 2017 | Namibia | Nationally representative household survey ($N = 4,000+$) | Complex survey design cross-tabulations | Urban/rural location, income, credit strands, reasons for non-borrowing | Only 12.8% of adults use bank credit; 57.9% do not borrow; 95.1% cite 'fear of debt'; rural exclusion is 27.1% vs 17.5% urban. | Cross-sectional; 2017 data precedes recent mobile wallet expansion (e.g., MTC Maris). | Primary official empirical benchmark for Namibian credit exclusion dynamics. | National Official Statistics Report |
| **Bank of Namibia & NAMFISA** | 2024 | Namibia | National commercial banking regulatory filings | Prudential regulatory assessment | NPL ratios, capital adequacy, credit extension, household debt | Non-performing loans reached 5.9% in 2023 due to elevated interest rates; individuals dominate credit default volume. | Aggregated banking sector indicators; no borrower-level microdata. | Official benchmark for baseline default rates in the Namibian financial sector. | National Central Bank Report |
| **Bank of Namibia** | 2026b | Namibia | National banking supervisory records (as of Dec 2025) | Macro-prudential stability monitoring | Sectoral NPLs, loan write-offs, household debt-to-income | NPL ratio declined to 4.3% by end-2025; individuals account for 60.2% of total NPLs, while hospitality accounts for 18.7%. | Aggregated macro ratios; no individual loan logs. | Sets current empirical baseline default distribution (4.3%–5.9%) for model calibration. | National Central Bank Report |
